Slab Leak Warning Signs in Jacksonville Homes (and What to Do Next)

October 18, 2026 · 7 min read

Direct answer: A slab leak is a water line leaking underneath your home's concrete foundation. Watch for warm spots on the floor, an unexplained spike in your water bill, the sound of running water when nothing's on, cracks in flooring or walls, and musty smells near the floor. Slab leaks are common in Jacksonville's slab-on-grade homes — especially older ones with original copper lines. Confirm with your water meter, then call a plumber for detection; restoration handles the water damage the leak caused.

Most Jacksonville homes sit on a concrete slab with water lines running underneath it — out of sight, under pressure, 24 hours a day. When one of those lines fails, you won't see a dramatic flood. You'll see a slow, quiet leak that can run for weeks before anyone notices. Here's how to catch it early.

What exactly is a slab leak?

Direct answer: A slab leak is a leak in the pressurized water supply lines (or sometimes drain lines) buried in or beneath your home's concrete foundation slab. Because the pipes are encased in concrete and soil, the leak has nowhere visible to go — so water spreads under the slab, wicks up through flooring, and erodes soil beneath the foundation.

Jacksonville's housing stock makes this a local issue: huge numbers of homes here are slab-on-grade construction, many built from the 1960s through the 1990s with copper supply lines. Copper corrodes over decades — Jacksonville's soil chemistry and occasional shifting can accelerate it. Newer homes with PEX lines are far less vulnerable, which is why slab leaks skew toward older neighborhoods. (For how supply lines fail in winter, see our guide on burst pipes in Jacksonville.)

What are the warning signs?

Direct answer: The classic signs are a hot spot on the floor (hot-water line leak), a water bill that jumps with no change in usage, the sound of running water when everything's off, damp carpet or warped flooring, cracks appearing in walls or flooring, and low water pressure.

  • Warm spots on the floor — Walk barefoot across tile or wood floors. A distinct warm patch usually means a hot-water line is leaking underneath, heating the slab.
  • Water bill spike — Compare this month to the same month last year. A jump of 20–30% or more with no lifestyle change is a red flag.
  • Running water sounds — Stand quietly near the floor in different rooms. Hearing water movement with all fixtures off is a strong indicator.
  • Damp or warped flooring — Moisture wicking up through the slab buckles wood, loosens tile, and dampens carpet — often in one localized area. (See when hardwood floors can be saved vs. replaced after water damage.)
  • Cracks in walls or flooring — Water eroding soil under the slab causes subtle settling; new cracks near the floor line deserve attention.
  • Musty smell at floor level — Persistent dampness under flooring grows mold and mildew long before you see it.
  • Low water pressure — A significant leak bleeds pressure from the whole system.
  • Water meter spinning — The definitive home test (see below).

How can you confirm it before calling anyone?

Direct answer: Do the water meter test: turn off every faucet, appliance, and irrigation zone, then watch your water meter for 30–60 minutes. If the dial or leak indicator moves with zero water use, water is escaping somewhere — and a slab leak is a prime suspect.

Steps:

  1. Turn off all water inside and outside the house (including ice makers and irrigation).
  2. Find your water meter (usually near the curb) and note the reading — or watch the small leak-indicator dial.
  3. Wait 30–60 minutes without using any water.
  4. Check again. Movement = a leak somewhere in your system.

This test confirms a leak, not where it is. Pinpointing a slab leak takes acoustic listening equipment or thermal imaging — that's plumber territory.

Who do you call — a plumber or a restoration company?

Direct answer: Call a plumber first. Finding and repairing the leaking pipe — whether by spot repair, rerouting the line overhead, or repiping — is plumbing work. A restoration company comes in after, to handle the water damage the leak caused: drying, damaged flooring, and any mold growth.

The order matters. There's no point drying a floor while the pipe underneath is still leaking. A good sequence:

  1. Plumber: electronic leak detection, then repair (spot fix vs. reroute — rerouting overhead avoids breaking the slab).
  2. Restoration: moisture mapping, structural drying, flooring repair/replacement, mold remediation if the leak ran long.
  3. Insurance: document everything from day one — photos, the plumber's report, the water bills showing the spike.

Does homeowners insurance cover slab leaks?

Direct answer: Usually partially. Most Florida policies cover the water damage caused by the leak (torn-up flooring, drying, repairs to the home) but exclude the cost of fixing the pipe itself and often exclude damage from long-term seepage. Sudden, accidental leaks are covered; slow leaks you ignored for months may not be.

This is why the water-bill documentation matters — it helps establish when the leak started. And it's why catching it early isn't just about repair costs; it's about keeping the damage inside your policy's coverage. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does slab leak repair cost?

Detection typically runs a few hundred dollars. The repair itself varies widely: a spot repair through the slab is generally the cheapest option, while rerouting lines overhead or a full repipe costs more but avoids breaking concrete. The water damage restoration afterward is a separate cost — get separate quotes for each.

Can a slab leak cause foundation damage?

Yes, over time. Water eroding soil beneath the slab causes settling and voids, which can crack the slab itself. This is a slow process — weeks to months — which is exactly why early detection matters.

Are slab leaks covered if I have a home warranty?

Sometimes partially. Many home warranties cover the plumbing repair but with caps and exclusions. Read your specific contract — "plumbing" coverage varies enormously between warranty companies.

How long can a slab leak go unnoticed?

Weeks to months. Hot-water line leaks get noticed faster (warm floors, higher energy bills). Cold-water line leaks are sneakier — the water bill is often the first clue.

Should I file an insurance claim for a slab leak?

If the resulting water damage is substantial — damaged flooring across rooms, mold remediation needed — usually yes. For the pipe repair alone, most policies won't cover it, so weigh your deductible against the damage cost before filing.
