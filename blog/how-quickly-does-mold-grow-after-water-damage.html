How Quickly Does Mold Grow After Water Damage? (24–48 Hours)

November 24, 2026 · 7 min read

You mopped the floor. You ran a fan all night. The room looks dry, so you close the door and move on. Two days later there is a sour, earthy smell coming from the baseboard, and it is getting stronger, not weaker.

That smell is the part most Jacksonville homeowners get wrong. Mold does not wait a week. After water damage, it can start growing in 24 to 48 hours — often before you see a single spot. After a leak in Riverside, a burst pipe in Mandarin, or storm water in San Marco, the question is how much time you have left before a drying job turns into a mold remediation job.

How quickly does mold grow after water damage?

Mold can start growing in 24 to 48 hours on a wet, warm surface. That is the germination window: the time a dormant spore, already sitting in your home, needs to wake up, put out tiny root-like threads, and start feeding.

You will not see it at that stage. What you see later — the black, green, or grey patch on drywall — is a colony already growing for days.

Spores alone are not the problem. They are in every house in Duval County, in the air and in dust. They do nothing until water arrives. When a supply line leaks behind a wall in Springfield or a water heater lets go in Arlington, spores land on wet material and the clock starts. It does not pause while you wait for a weekend or an insurance call back.

What does mold need to grow inside your home?

Mold needs three things: moisture, food, and the right temperature. Take away any one of them and growth stops. Leave all three in place and it will not.

Moisture is the one you control. Mold needs a surface that stays damp, not one that dried in an hour. A tile floor you towel off is low risk. A carpet pad that stays soaked under the carpet is not.

Food is already built into your house: drywall paper, wood, carpet backing, dust, flooring glue. You cannot starve mold out. You have to dry it out.

Temperature is easy for mold in Florida. It grows best in roughly the range you keep your house, 60 to 90 degrees. A slow leak under a kitchen sink in Orange Park gives mold all three at once: moisture, cabinet wood and drywall paper for food, and warm, still air behind a closed door.

Why does Jacksonville humidity keep materials in the growth zone longer?

Jacksonville humidity keeps wet materials damp far longer than the same materials would stay wet in a dry climate. Indoor air in many local homes runs 60 to 70 percent humidity even with no leak at all.

Drying is a race between the material and the air around it. Dry air pulls moisture out of wet drywall. Humid air does not — a wet wall has almost nowhere to send its water.

Homes near the St. Johns River, Trout River, and the creeks through San Marco and Riverside feel this most after heavy rain and high tides. The ground stays wet, crawl spaces stay damp, and indoor air stays heavy long after the rain stops. Closed-up rooms make it worse: a spare bedroom in Southside with the door shut, a bathroom with no working fan, a laundry room in Westside with a slow hose leak. Materials that might dry in a day in an open room can stay in the growth zone for three or four days here. Your air conditioner helps, but it was built to cool air, not dry a soaked wall cavity.

What does the mold growth timeline look like day by day?

The timeline runs like this: germination in 24 to 48 hours, visible colonies in 3 to 7 days on fast-growing materials, and wider spread in 1 to 2 weeks if the moisture stays.

First 24 hours. Water spreads further than you can see. Drywall wicks it up from the floor line, and carpet pad holds it like a sponge under carpet that feels only slightly damp on top.

24 to 48 hours. Spores germinate. Threads grow into drywall paper, wood, and pad, and start feeding. There is usually no visible patch yet and often no smell. This is the cheapest moment to stop the whole problem — and the one most people miss, because nothing looks wrong.

3 to 7 days. Colonies become visible on the fastest materials, above all drywall paper: small dark spots along a baseboard, bubbling paint, a musty smell at the door. Behind the wall, growth is usually ahead of what shows.

1 to 2 weeks. Colonies spread to nearby damp materials — the next sheet of drywall, a cabinet bottom, the subfloor, wall insulation — and each one releases more spores to start again elsewhere. You no longer have a drying job alone. A pinhole leak that keeps one section of wall damp for a week follows this same timeline, in a smaller area.

Which materials grow mold fastest, and which resist it?

Drywall paper, carpet pad, and insulation grow mold fastest. Tile, concrete, and solid metal resist it, because mold has little or nothing to eat on those surfaces.

Drywall is the biggest concern in most Jacksonville homes. Its gypsum core is covered in paper, and that paper is an ideal food source. Water wicks up inside the wall, the back face stays wet and hidden, and mold grows where you cannot see or clean it. Drywall wet past 48 hours often has to come out rather than be saved.

Carpet pad is next. It holds a lot of water, sits in the dark under carpet, and gets no air. Carpet can sometimes be cleaned and dried; pad soaked for days usually cannot, and is normally removed. Wet insulation is similar — it holds moisture against the wood and drywall around it, slowing drying for everything nearby.

Wood sits in the middle. Solid framing can often be dried in place if work starts early; particleboard and cheap cabinet board swell and feed growth sooner. Tile, concrete block, and concrete floors can be cleaned and dried rather than removed, though mold can grow on dirt or soap film on them. A slab home in Jacksonville Beach with tile throughout faces a very different risk from a raised home with carpet, pad, and drywall at floor level.

What should you do in the first hours after water damage?

Stop the water, get standing water out, and get drying started the same day. Drying speed is the whole game, because every step that beats the 24 to 48 hour window keeps mold from starting at all.

Shut off the source if you can do it safely, at the fixture or at the main. If water is near outlets or appliances and you are not sure it is safe, stay out and call for help. Then lift furniture off wet carpet and move rugs, boxes, and paper goods off wet floors — paper and cardboard feed growth and hold water down.

Take date-stamped photos of the source, the water, and the damaged materials before you throw anything out. They help with an insurance claim and cost two minutes.

Do not rely on a fan and hope. A fan moves air across a surface; it does not pull water out of a wall cavity, a pad, or insulation, and in humid Jacksonville air it takes far too long. Proper structural drying uses extraction, placed air movers, and commercial dehumidifiers, with progress checked by moisture meter — not by touching the wall and guessing. Do not seal a wet area up either: closing a cabinet or painting over a damp stain traps moisture in the dark, where mold grows undisturbed.

If the water came from a toilet overflow, a sewage backup, or outside flooding, treat it as contaminated, keep children and pets out, and do not clean it with household products alone — see sewage cleanup for how that work is handled. Frequently Asked Questions

How quickly does mold grow after water damage?

Mold can germinate in 24 to 48 hours on wet material. Visible colonies usually appear in 3 to 7 days on drywall paper and other fast-growing surfaces, and spread widens over 1 to 2 weeks if the moisture is not removed.

Can mold grow if the surface feels dry to the touch?

Yes. A surface can feel dry while the paper backing inside the wall, the insulation, or the carpet pad underneath is still wet. That hidden moisture is where growth starts, which is why moisture meter readings matter more than a touch test.

Will bleach stop mold from growing after a leak?

No. Bleach can lighten a surface stain, but it does not dry the material and does not reach growth deep in drywall or wood. If the material stays wet, growth returns. Drying or removing it is what stops mold.

How long do you have to dry a home before mold becomes a bigger job?

Aim for extraction and drying within 24 hours. Past 48 hours of wet material, the chance of needing removal and remediation rises sharply — typically $1,500–$4,500 for mold remediation in a typical home, on top of drying.
